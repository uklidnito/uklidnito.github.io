Supplements & other paths to calm

Breathing is the foundation, but a few other things help with calm too. A short overview of what research actually says about them.

Magnesium

Magnesium has recently become one of the most popular supplements for calm, largely thanks to social media. Reviews show that in people prone to anxiety or with lower magnesium levels, supplementing may slightly improve feelings of calm and sleep quality, though the evidence is not yet strong — studies tend to be small and use different forms and doses. It's a cheap, safe supplement with minimal side effects, so it's worth trying, ideally after checking with a doctor (especially for people with kidney issues).

L-theanine

An amino acid from green tea that, according to EEG studies, increases relaxed but alert alpha brain waves without causing drowsiness. In small studies, a dose of around 200 mg before a stressful situation slightly reduced anxiety and the body's stress response. Unlike classic sedatives, it doesn't slow reaction time or affect memory, and combined with caffeine (like in a cup of tea) it helps ease the jitters caffeine can cause on its own.

Cold showers

Cold first triggers a brief stress shock — heart rate and breathing speed up for a few seconds and stress hormones are released. Only afterward does the vagus nerve activate and calm set in: heart rate drops below baseline and the nervous system shifts into a calmer mode. A 2022 study confirmed that applying cold to the face after a stressful task calmed heart rate and subjective stress faster than without the cold. People with heart problems, high blood pressure, or who are pregnant should only try cold exposure after consulting a doctor.

Caffeine and anxiety

The relationship between caffeine and anxiety isn't clear-cut. At smaller doses, and in people used to regular consumption, caffeine often improves focus and mood; at higher doses or in more sensitive people, it raises cortisol and can trigger or worsen nervousness and a racing heart. If you're prone to anxiety or sleep poorly, it can help to limit caffeine to the morning and watch how your body responds — for some, even one extra cup is enough to bring on restlessness.

Progressive muscle relaxation

Edmund Jacobson's method from the 1930s works by progressively tensing and releasing individual muscle groups from the feet up to the face — the body learns to better recognize the difference between tension and release, which helps it notice and let go of hidden tension we don't usually notice. Recent reviews confirm that regular practice reduces anxiety, stress and depressive symptoms, including in older adults or those in hospital care. A session usually lasts 5 to 20 minutes and can be done anywhere, sitting or lying down.

Mindfulness meditation

Mindfulness means consciously and non-judgmentally focusing attention on the present moment — most often the breath, bodily sensations, or sounds around you. The best-known and best-studied program is the eight-week MBSR course, created in the 1970s by Jon Kabat-Zinn. A large 2014 meta-analysis of dozens of studies found that meditation programs lead to a mild but consistent improvement in anxiety, depression and pain perception, comparable to other active therapies.

Time in nature

According to attention restoration theory, cities flood the brain with stimuli you must actively react to, while nature captures attention gently and lets the brain rest. A 2015 study found that a 90-minute walk in nature, compared to a walk through a busy street, reduced activity in a brain region linked to dwelling on negative thoughts. More recent research found that even 20-30 minutes in green space can measurably lower cortisol — it doesn't have to be wilderness, a city park works too.

Alcohol and sleep

Alcohol initially depresses the nervous system, so it can seem like it helps you fall asleep faster — but that effect is deceptive. As it's metabolized overnight, sleep becomes fragmented, you wake up more often, and REM sleep, during which emotions are processed and memory consolidated, is significantly suppressed. Alcohol also relaxes throat muscles, worsening snoring and raising the risk of sleep apnea. Regular drinking before bed is therefore linked long-term to worse sleep quality, more fatigue and higher anxiety — the opposite of what people look for when reaching for a drink "to relax."

Nicotine

A cigarette or vape is often seen as a way to calm down, but nicotine is actually a stimulant — it speeds up heart rate, raises blood pressure and stimulates the nervous system, similar to caffeine. The sense of relief mostly comes from withdrawal symptoms subsiding between cigarettes, not from any real calming effect. Smokers have, on average, higher anxiety and stress than non-smokers, and anxiety declines after quitting. Nicotine affects sleep much like caffeine — shortening it and causing more frequent waking.

Sleep hygiene

Good sleep rests mainly on consistency — the internal clock sets itself best when you go to bed and wake up around the same time, even on weekends. Two environmental factors have solid research support: a cooler bedroom (around 18-20°C), since body temperature naturally drops as you fall asleep, and darkness, since light suppresses melatonin production via the retina. It's recommended to limit bright light and screens 1-3 hours before bed and sleep in a dark room — paired with a breathing exercise, this can make a good evening ritual.

Diet and adequate protein

Protein supplies tryptophan, needed to produce serotonin, and also slows the absorption of sugar from food — helping prevent blood-sugar swings that can mimic anxiety (shakiness, a racing heart, irritability). A healthy adult needs roughly 0.8-1.2 g of protein per kilogram of body weight per day, spread across meals. Watch out especially for highly processed foods and excess added sugar, which recent studies link to a higher risk of depression and anxiety.

Sugar and dopamine

Fast-absorbing sugar triggers dopamine release in the brain's reward pathway — the same one activated by addictive substances — so sweets bring a quick wave of good mood. A few hours later, though, blood sugar drops and mood drops with it, leading to cravings for more sweets; protein and fiber ease this crash. In animals, repeated access to large amounts of sugar leads to tolerance-like effects and fewer dopamine receptors; in humans, the question of "sugar addiction" is still debated, but high sugar intake is repeatedly linked to higher rates of depression in studies.

Exercise and movement

The relationship between exercise and anxiety is one of the best-studied topics in sports psychology — even a single bout of aerobic exercise can briefly reduce anxiety, and regular physical activity lowers anxiety even in the general population. Not all movement works the same way: a more recent meta-analysis found mind-body exercise paired with conscious breathing, like yoga or tai chi, most effective, followed by classic aerobic exercise. Consistency matters too — programs shorter than 10 weeks didn't significantly reduce anxiety, while longer ones did.

Omega-3 fatty acids

Omega-3s (EPA and DHA) from oily fish or fish oil are among the most studied supplements when it comes to mood. A large 2024 meta-analysis found that anxiety symptoms decreased slightly as the daily dose increased, with the strongest effect around 2 grams per day, though the certainty of the evidence is still low. Omega-3s are generally well tolerated; at higher doses (above roughly 3 g per day) they can slightly prolong bleeding time, which matters mainly for people on blood-thinning medication — worth discussing dosage with a doctor in that case.

L-tryptophan for calmer sleep

L-tryptophan is an essential amino acid the body can't make on its own — it has to come from food such as turkey, chicken, eggs, cheese, legumes or pumpkin seeds. It's the raw material for serotonin and, further down the line, melatonin, so it plays a role in both mood and sleep-wake regulation. A 2022 meta-analysis found that supplemental doses of 1 gram or more can shorten the time it takes to fall asleep and improve overall sleep quality. Don't combine L-tryptophan with antidepressants (SSRIs, MAOIs) without talking to a doctor first — the combination carries a risk of serotonin syndrome.

Essential vitamins

Vitamins are organic compounds that the body cannot make in sufficient amounts on its own, so they must come from food. They fall into two groups: fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K), which the body can store, and water-soluble vitamins (the B-complex and vitamin C), which need to be replenished more regularly. Vitamins support energy production, immunity, bone health, the nervous system and skin. A deficiency can show up as fatigue, weaker immunity, or skin and mucous-membrane problems, which is why a varied diet is the foundation of prevention.

Creatine

Creatine is a compound the body partly makes itself from amino acids and partly obtains from food (meat, fish) — most of it is stored in muscle, where it acts as a fast energy source for short, intense effort. A smaller amount is also found in the brain, which is why recent research has looked at its effect on brain-cell energetics, mental fatigue and mood. Several smaller studies suggest that creatine, added to therapy or antidepressants, may ease depressive symptoms, though the evidence is still preliminary and not every study has confirmed the effect.

Melatonin and the sleep cycle

Melatonin is a hormone the body makes on its own in the pineal gland once it gets dark — it's the main signal the body uses to tell itself that sleep time is approaching. As a supplement, it's mainly used to shift or realign the internal biological clock, for example with jet lag or delayed sleep phase, while the evidence for ordinary insomnia is less clear-cut. Unlike classic sleeping pills, it doesn't work as a sedative but more as a signal for the body that the day is really ending — and the same signal is, in turn, suppressed by evening light from screens.

Morning calm and daily priorities

The first few minutes after waking shape how the whole day feels. Before reaching for the phone, it helps to take 3–5 minutes of quiet, let the body properly wake up, and briefly sum up in your head what the day holds. It also helps to narrow the to-do list down to just two or three key tasks — the brain can only handle a limited number of clear priorities, and the more tasks we load onto ourselves first thing in the morning, the sooner decision fatigue sets in. Even a short written plan calms the mind, because unfinished tasks stop nagging in the background.
